Four ways a Portuguese property gets used, four different policies

Almost every problem in this part of the market comes from a policy bought for one of these situations and used in another. Insurers underwrite the use, not the building. Find the column you are in.

Owner-occupied

  • You live there.
  • Standard multi-risk home policy.
  • Your building, your contents, your liability.
  • See home insurance.

Second home

  • Used by you and unpaid guests.
  • No income from the property.
  • Home policy with the occupancy pattern declared.
  • See second home insurance.

Long-term rental

  • A tenant lives there under a residential lease.
  • You insure the building, your fixtures and your liability as owner.
  • The tenant’s belongings are not yours to insure.
  • See rental property insurance.

Holiday rental / AL

  • Paying guests, short stays, constant turnover.
  • Registered under the Alojamento Local regime.
  • Different obligations and a different risk profile again.
  • See AL insurance requirements.

If a property moves between columns during the year — family in August, holiday guests in July, empty in February — say so. That pattern is entirely normal and entirely insurable, but only if the insurer knows about it.

What a landlord actually needs to insure

A landlord policy in Portugal is built from the same set of multi-risk covers as a home policy, assembled around a different question: not “what do I lose if this burns down” but “what am I responsible for while someone else lives here”.

  • The building. Structure, fixtures, fitted kitchens and bathrooms, insured on a rebuild basis. This is the core of the policy and it is your responsibility, not the tenant’s.
  • Landlord’s contents. What you supplied: white goods, furniture in a furnished let, curtains, air conditioning. Not the tenant’s possessions.
  • Owner’s liability. Third-party claims arising from the property — a defective installation, a falling balcony rail, water reaching the flat below. Explained in full here.
  • Water damage. Both to your property and, importantly, to the neighbours it reaches. The most common claim in Portuguese rentals.
  • Fire, storm and impact. The standard perils, with the same rebuild-value logic as any home policy.
  • Seismic cover. Optional in Portugal and frequently missing from older policies. Worth checking on a let property too.
  • Loss of rent. Where available, income lost while the property is genuinely uninhabitable after an insured event. This is not the same thing as a tenant who stops paying.
  • Empty periods. Between tenants, the property is unoccupied and the policy usually treats it differently. See unoccupied property cover.

Everything on this page is general guidance. Sub-limits, excesses, occupancy conditions and exclusions differ between insurers and between policies, and the wording of your own policy is what decides a claim.

What landlord insurance does not do

Being straight about this saves everyone time, and it is the part most owners arriving from the UK or Ireland expect to work differently.

  • It does not insure the tenant’s belongings. Their furniture, electronics and clothes are theirs to insure. Many leases require them to hold their own contents policy; that is a matter for the lease, not for your policy.
  • It is not a rent guarantee. Loss of rent cover, where it exists, responds when the property cannot be lived in after an insured loss. It does not respond to a tenant who simply stops paying. More on that below.
  • It does not cover wear and tear. Ageing installations, tired paint and general deterioration are maintenance, not insured perils.
  • It does not replace legal process. Recovering possession of a property in Portugal is a legal matter with its own timetable. No policy shortens it.
  • It does not cover undeclared letting. A property let on a policy that describes it as owner-occupied is the single most common reason a landlord claim runs into trouble.

Rent guarantee and landlord legal expenses: an honest answer

These two come up in almost every conversation with a British or Irish landlord, because in those markets they are standard bolt-ons. In Portugal the picture is different, and rather than publish a page pretending otherwise, here is the position as it actually stands.

  • Rent guarantee. Products addressing non-payment of rent do exist in the Portuguese market, but availability, eligibility criteria and tenant vetting requirements vary considerably and they are not a routine extension of a landlord policy. We will tell you honestly what we can and cannot obtain for your specific situation rather than quoting something generic. Ask us, and we will come back with a straight answer.
  • Landlord legal expenses. Legal expenses cover exists in Portugal and is more commonly written for individuals and households than as a landlord-specific product. Where a policy we can access extends to disputes arising from a let property, we will say so; where it does not, we will say that instead. There is background on how legal expenses cover works here: personal legal expenses cover in Portugal.
  • Deposits and lease terms. The deposit, the guarantor and the lease wording remain the primary protections against non-payment for most Portuguese landlords. Those are legal instruments rather than insurance ones, and your lawyer is the right person for them.

We are a broker, not a product manufacturer. If we cannot place something we say so; we would rather lose a quote than sell you a policy that does not do what you thought it did.

Practical points for landlords who live abroad

Frequently asked questions

Do I need landlord insurance to rent out a property in Portugal?

Buildings in a condominium regime must carry fire cover by law, and a mortgage lender will require insurance. Beyond that, letting without cover for the building and for your liability as owner leaves you personally exposed to losses that can run well past the value of the rent.

Can I just keep my normal home insurance and put a tenant in?

No, and this is the most common mistake we see. A home policy is underwritten on the basis that the owner occupies the property. Letting it without telling the insurer is a change of risk, and it is the sort of thing that surfaces during a claim rather than before one.

Does landlord insurance cover my tenant’s belongings?

No. Your policy covers the building, anything you supplied, and your liability as owner. The tenant’s own possessions are theirs to insure, and many leases require them to do so.

What happens between tenants when the property is empty?

The property becomes unoccupied, and most policies treat unoccupied periods differently — typically with conditions attached and some covers, especially escape of water, restricted. Insurers define this in their own wording rather than by a national rule. Tell us when a vacancy is likely to run long.

Can I insure a Portuguese rental property if I live in the UK or Ireland?

Yes. Non-resident landlords insure Portuguese property routinely. You will need a Portuguese tax number, and everything else — quote, policy, renewal, claim — we handle in English from wherever you are.

Is rent guarantee insurance available in Portugal?

Products addressing non-payment exist in the market, but they are not a routine extension of a landlord policy and eligibility varies. We will tell you honestly what we can obtain for your situation instead of implying it is a standard bolt-on.

What if I let the property to holiday guests instead?

That is the Alojamento Local regime, and it is a different risk with different obligations. A long-term landlord policy is generally not the right contract for short-stay guests. Tell us which you are doing, or if you are doing both.

The property is owned by a company. Does that matter?

Yes. The policyholder is the company rather than you, which affects the documentation, the insurable interest and sometimes the insurers available. It is entirely workable; it just needs to be set up correctly from the start.

How much does landlord insurance cost in Portugal?

It depends on the rebuild value, the location, the construction, the letting type and the covers chosen, so a single figure would be dishonest. A let property is not automatically more expensive than an owner-occupied one, and comparing insurers moves the number more than most owners expect.
