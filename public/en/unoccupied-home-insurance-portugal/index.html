Unoccupied property insurance in Portugal

An empty house is not an unloved house. It is a renovation, an inheritance, a gap between tenants, or a purchase waiting for its owner to retire. Insurers treat all of them as a different risk from an occupied home — and most standard policies say so somewhere in the wording.

Who this is for

Properties end up empty for ordinary reasons. Someone dies and the estate takes eighteen months to settle. A tenant leaves in November and the next one signs in March. A renovation runs long. An owner in Dublin or Düsseldorf buys the house they will retire to in four years’ time. In every one of those cases the building still needs insuring, and in every one of them the standard assumption behind a home policy — that somebody is living there — has stopped being true.

Between tenants

A rental property with a vacancy running longer than the usual few weeks.

Under renovation

Works in progress, contractors on site, the house uninhabitable in the meantime.

Inherited property

An estate not yet settled, heirs abroad, nobody with the time to be there.

Bought but not moved into

A purchase made years before the move, sitting empty and largely unfurnished.

For sale

A property on the market that has already been emptied of its owner and its furniture.

Owner living abroad

A house visited so rarely that the empty state is the normal state.

Why insurers treat empty property differently

The risks are not exotic. They are the same risks any house faces, with the person who would have noticed them removed. A dripping joint becomes a saturated floor. A loose tile becomes a wet ceiling over a winter. A break-in in an occupied street is interrupted; in an empty house it is thorough.

  • Escape of water. Consistently the largest cause of loss in empty properties, and the cover most likely to be limited or excluded while a property is unoccupied.
  • Theft and vandalism. Copper pipe, boilers, air-conditioning units and solar equipment are stolen from empty houses. Squatting and casual damage are less common but not unknown.
  • Undetected deterioration. Damp, mould and rot are usually excluded as gradual causes in any case, but an empty house is where they get the time to develop.
  • Storm damage found late. Roof damage in January, discovered in April, with four months of rain in between.
  • Liability. The building still exists in relation to neighbours and the street. A falling tile or a leak into the flat below does not wait for occupancy.

There is no universal number of days. Portugal does not set a national threshold at which a home becomes “unoccupied”. Each insurer defines it in its own wording, with its own period and its own conditions, and those definitions genuinely differ from one contract to another. Anybody who tells you the answer is 30, 60 or 90 days is quoting one insurer and calling it a rule. We read your actual wording and tell you what yours says.

What can usually be arranged, and what usually cannot

Cover for an empty property is generally narrower than cover for an occupied one, and the narrowing is deliberate rather than punitive. What is available depends on the insurer, the reason the property is empty and how long it will stay that way.

Often available

  • Fire, lightning and explosion.
  • Storm and impact damage to the building.
  • Liability arising from the property.
  • Theft, frequently with security conditions attached.
  • Seismic cover, where the policy includes it.

Often restricted

  • Escape of water during the unoccupied period.
  • Malicious damage and vandalism.
  • Contents, particularly anything portable or valuable.
  • Glass breakage.
  • Higher excesses than an occupied property would carry.

Usually conditional

  • Water supply shut off and systems drained.
  • Periodic inspection by a named person, and a record of it.
  • Alarm set and maintained where one exists.
  • The property kept secure, tidy and visibly attended.
  • The insurer told before the empty period begins, not after.

Everything on this page is general guidance. Sub-limits, excesses, occupancy conditions and exclusions differ between insurers and between policies, and the wording of your own policy is what decides a claim.

If the property is being renovated

Renovation changes the question again. A home policy is written for a finished, habitable building; a building site with contractors, scaffolding and an open roof is not that. Depending on the scale of the works, the right answer may be a home policy with the works declared and endorsed, or it may be a different contract altogether alongside the contractor’s own insurance.

  • Tell the insurer before works start. Not when the claim happens. Structural work carried out without notice is a familiar reason for a declined claim.
  • Check what the builder carries. The contractor’s liability and, on larger projects, their own construction cover. Ask for the certificate rather than the reassurance.
  • Structural works have their own regime. On substantial building projects Portuguese rules can bring other insurances into play — we explain that here.
  • Listed and heritage buildings. Reinstatement obligations can change the sum insured materially. See renovating a listed property.

Practical steps that keep cover intact

Turn the water off

At the mains, and drain down where the property will be empty over winter. It removes the single biggest cause of loss.

Arrange a key-holder

A named person who can enter, inspect and let a loss adjuster in. Many insurers ask who this is.

Keep a record of inspections

A dated note or a photo each visit. If cover depends on inspection, evidence of it is what settles the argument.

Do not let it look empty

Post piling up, an overgrown garden and permanently closed shutters advertise the fact.

Tell us when it changes

When a tenant moves in, works finish, or the estate settles, the risk changes and so should the policy.

Keep the alarm working

A declared alarm that has been unplugged for a year is worse than no alarm at all.

What we need to prepare your quote

  1. Why it is empty. Between tenants, renovation, inheritance, waiting for a move — insurers respond differently to each.
  2. How long, realistically. An honest estimate is worth more than an optimistic one.
  3. The property itself. Address or postcode, type, size, year built, condition, and whether it is furnished.
  4. Security and access. Locks, shutters, alarm, and who holds a key locally.

Frequently asked questions

How long can a property be empty before it counts as unoccupied?

There is no national rule in Portugal. Each insurer sets its own definition and its own conditions in the policy wording, and they differ meaningfully between contracts. The only answer that protects you is the one written in your own policy, which we read and explain before you sign.

Will my existing home insurance still cover an empty house?

It may, in a reduced form, and it may not. Most Portuguese multi-risk policies contain a clause dealing with unoccupied periods, and the usual effect is that some covers — escape of water in particular — become restricted or fall away. Send us the policy and we will tell you exactly where yours stands.

Do I have to tell the insurer the property is empty?

Yes. Occupancy is material to the risk. An insurer that finds out at claim stage that a property had been empty for a year without notice is entitled to take that into account, and the outcome is rarely in the owner’s favour.

Can I insure a property that is being renovated?

Usually, but the works have to be declared and the right structure depends on their scale. Cosmetic work is often handled by endorsement; substantial structural work may need a different contract alongside the builder’s own insurance.

What about a property I have inherited?

Inherited property can be insured while the estate is being settled. We will need to know who the legal owners are, or who is acting for the estate, and it helps to sort the cover early rather than after the first winter of nobody checking.

Is theft covered in an empty house?

Often, but usually with conditions about locks, shutters and alarms, and sometimes with a lower limit than an occupied property would carry. Insurers vary considerably here, which is precisely why comparing wordings rather than prices is worth the effort.

Does turning off the water really make a difference?

A large one. Escape of water is the most common and most expensive loss in empty properties, and some insurers make shutting off the supply during unoccupied periods an explicit condition of cover rather than a suggestion.

What happens when the property is occupied again?

Tell us and we adjust the policy. A property that returns to full occupancy usually goes back onto ordinary home terms, and there is no reason to keep paying for a restricted contract once the restriction no longer applies.
