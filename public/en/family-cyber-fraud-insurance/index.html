Family cyber, fraud and identity insurance

The most expensive cyber event a private family is likely to suffer is not a hacked laptop. It is a payment diverted to a fraudster — a property deposit, a completion payment, a builder’s invoice — after an email that looked exactly like the lawyer’s. In Portugal and Spain, where international buyers routinely transfer large sums during a property purchase, this happens often.

We arrange family cyber and fraud cover, as an extension to a high-value home policy or as a standalone policy, placed with insurers, specialist markets and co-brokerage partners who write personal cyber risks.

Request a written assessment WhatsApp

Who it is for

Families whose financial life runs through email, messaging and online banking — which is nearly all of them.

  • Families buying, selling or building property in Portugal or Spain, with large transfers to lawyers, sellers and contractors
  • Households with smart-home systems, connected security and many devices
  • Families with a public profile, and children and teenagers active online
  • Owners of several homes, where staff and managers also send and receive payment instructions

The risks, and what differs between Portugal and Spain

The common events are the same in both countries: payment-diversion fraud (an intercepted or spoofed email changes the bank details), impersonation of a family member or adviser on messaging apps, ransomware on personal devices, account takeover, identity theft used to open credit or accounts, and harassment or reputational attacks online. What differs is mostly practical:

 PortugalSpain
Property transactionsDeposits under the promissory contract (CPCV) and completion funds often move by transfer between parties and lawyersDeposits under the arras contract and completion payments, usually around the notary appointment
ReportingReport to the bank at once, and to the Polícia Judiciária’s cybercrime unitReport to the bank at once, and to the Policía Nacional or Guardia Civil cybercrime units
Data protectionGDPR, supervised by the CNPDGDPR, supervised by the AEPD

One habit prevents most payment-diversion losses: before any significant transfer, confirm the bank details by telephone with the recipient, on a number you already know — never one given in the email that asks for the payment.

What a well-placed policy includes

Personal cyber wordings vary widely. These are the sections that matter most.

Social engineering and payment fraud

Loss of funds transferred as a result of deception, including diverted deposit or completion payments — often sub-limited, so the limit must be checked.

Cyber extortion and ransomware

Response costs and, where lawful, extortion payments after ransomware or threats to release data from personal devices or the home network.

Identity theft restoration

Costs of restoring identity and credit records, legal costs, and lost time.

Online harassment and reputation

Support after cyberbullying or online attacks, including legal advice, psychological support and reputational specialists.

Data and device restoration

Recovering data and restoring devices and systems after malware or a cyber attack.

Smart-home systems

Restoring connected security, heating and home-automation systems after an attack.

24/7 incident response

Immediate access to specialists who help contain the incident and preserve evidence.

What usually goes wrong

  • A fraud limit far below the transfer. Funds-transfer fraud is often sub-limited well below the size of a property payment. The limit should match the transactions the family actually makes.
  • Assuming the bank will refund. An authorised transfer to a fraudster’s account is often not refunded by the bank, because the customer authorised it.
  • No cover for staff or managers. A household manager who pays a fraudulent invoice may fall outside the definition of insured person.
  • Waiting to report. Recovery chances fall quickly. Most policies also require prompt notification.
  • Treating it as an IT problem. The costliest events involve deception, not technology. The cover, and the family’s habits, should reflect that.

What we need from you to obtain terms

Personal cyber underwriting is short. A few answers make the difference.

  • Household members to be covered, and any staff who make payments on the family’s behalf
  • Whether a property purchase, sale or construction is planned, and the approximate size of the transfers involved
  • Basic security measures: multi-factor authentication, device updates, password manager
  • Smart-home systems in use
  • Any previous incidents, and any existing cyber extension on a home policy

Frequently asked questions

Does family cyber insurance cover a diverted property payment?
It can, under a social engineering or funds-transfer fraud section. These sections are often sub-limited, so the limit must be compared with the size of the payments you actually make. Conditions usually require that payment details were verified in a stated way.
Will my bank refund a fraudulent transfer?
Not necessarily. When the customer authorised the transfer, even if deceived, the bank may not be obliged to refund it. Recovery is sometimes possible if the fraud is reported immediately, which is why speed matters.
How can we reduce the risk of payment-diversion fraud?
Before any significant transfer, call the recipient on a telephone number you already know — not one in the email — and confirm the bank details. Treat any change of bank details by email as suspicious until confirmed that way.
Is this cover part of my home insurance?
Some high-value home policies include a family cyber extension; others offer none. Standalone cover is also available. We compare the limits and sub-limits of both before recommending one.
Does it help with online harassment of our children?
Many family cyber policies include support after cyberbullying or online harassment: specialists to remove content, legal advice and psychological support, and in some wordings the cost of changing schools.
