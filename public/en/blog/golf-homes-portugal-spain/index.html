Golf & Private Clients

Golf in Portugal and Spain: the great courses, the communities and the homes on the fairway

Published 27 September 2026 · 9 min read
Hugo Gonçalves
Founder & Risk Management Specialist · Adler & Rochefort

Few places in Europe let a golfer play in shirtsleeves in January, on courses as good as the light. Portugal and Spain have both. Between them they hold a Ryder Cup venue, a string of Tour stops, several of the most private clubs on the continent and some of the most sought-after residential communities anywhere — places where the house, the course and the club are, in practice, one decision.

This is a guide to that world: the destinations, the courses, the communities and the homes. At the end, briefly, we come to what a house on the fairway asks of its insurance — because a villa in La Zagaleta is not insured like a flat in town.

Why Iberia became Europe’s golf address

The answer is partly climate and partly patience. The Algarve’s first great courses — Penina, Vilamoura’s Old Course, Vale do Lobo — were laid out in the 1960s by developers who understood that golf would bring a certain kind of visitor, and that some would stay. On the Costa del Sol, Robert Trent Jones Sr. built Sotogrande and Las Brisas in the same era, and later reshaped Valderrama into the course that hosted the 1997 Ryder Cup, the first played in continental Europe.

What followed was not mass tourism but something quieter: clubs with waiting lists, estates with their own security and architecture committees, and families who have had a house on the same fairway for two generations.

Portugal: from the Algarve to the Atlantic islands

The Algarve’s Golden Triangle

Between Almancil and Vilamoura sits the country’s densest concentration of fine golf. Quinta do Lago has three courses — North, South and Laranjal — set among umbrella pines on the edge of the Ria Formosa, with some of the most valuable residential land in Portugal around them. Next door, Vale do Lobo offers the Royal and the Ocean, and the Royal’s clifftop par three over the ochre cliffs is one of the most photographed holes in European golf. Vilamoura, a few minutes west, has the Old Course, the town’s original layout among mature pines, and the Victoria, for many years the host of the Portugal Masters on the European Tour, last played in 2022.

Monte Rei and Palmares

At the two ends of the Algarve the tone changes. In the east, near Vila Nova de Cacela, Monte Rei is a Jack Nicklaus Signature course set back in the hills, consistently ranked among the best in the country and built around a private residential community with a very low density. In the west, Palmares in Lagos — a Robert Trent Jones Jr. design — runs from the dunes of Meia Praia up into the hills, with a view across the bay that few courses can match.

Comporta and the Alentejo coast

South of Lisbon, Comporta has become Portugal’s most discreet address, and its golf has followed. Comporta Dunas and Costa Terra, near Melides, are recent links-style courses routed through dunes and pine on one of the emptiest stretches of Atlantic coast in Europe. The houses are architect-designed, often in timber and reed, and usually second homes.

Cascais, Sintra and Estoril

Within half an hour of central Lisbon, Oitavos Dunes plays through the dunes of the Sintra-Cascais natural park with the Atlantic in view on almost every hole, Penha Longa winds through the hills below Sintra around a sixteenth-century monastery, and Quinta da Marinha sits inside one of the capital region’s most established residential estates.

The Silver Coast and Madeira

Around Óbidos, Praia d’El Rey, West Cliffs and Royal Óbidos offer seaside golf of real quality, cooler and quieter than the south. And in the Atlantic, Madeira has two courses of character: Palheiro, above Funchal, and Santo da Serra, laid out on a plateau with views to the Desertas islands and a former host of the Madeira Islands Open.

Spain: the Costa del Golf and beyond

The Costa del Sol

Between Sotogrande and Marbella lies what Spaniards call the Costa del Golf. Valderrama, the 1997 Ryder Cup venue, remains one of the most exclusive clubs in Europe; its neighbour, the Real Club de Golf Sotogrande, is the Trent Jones original from which the whole estate grew. Further east, Finca Cortésin at Casares has hosted the Volvo World Match Play and the 2023 Solheim Cup; La Zagaleta, in the hills above Benahávis, is a gated estate with two private courses and some of the most valuable homes in Spain; and Las Brisas, in Nueva Andalucía, anchors the “golf valley” behind Puerto Banús.

Mallorca

The island combines serious golf with a sailing culture. Son Muntaner, minutes from Palma, is the pick of the courses around the capital, and Alcanada, on the bay of Alcúdia with a lighthouse on its own islet, is widely considered one of the finest in Spain.

Catalonia, Madrid, the Canaries and the Costa Blanca

Near Girona, PGA Catalunya has hosted Tour events and draws golfers from across northern Europe to its Stadium course. In Madrid, golf is club life rather than a holiday: La Moraleja and the Real Club de la Puerta de Hierro sit among the capital’s most established residential areas, surrounded by family homes occupied all year. In Tenerife, Abama offers winter golf above the Atlantic with the island of La Gomera on the horizon, while on the Costa Blanca Las Colinas, near Orihuela, has built a residential community around a course that regularly ranks among Spain’s best.

The homes: living on the course

The property that grows around a great course has its own logic. On one side are the villas on the fairway — detached houses on generous plots, usually with a pool, gardens that merge into the course and a view the owner has paid a great deal for. On the other are gated and very private communities — La Zagaleta, Sotogrande, Quinta do Lago, Vale do Lobo, Monte Rei, Costa Terra — with controlled access, patrols, architectural guidelines and a level of service closer to a private estate than a housing development.

The community and its rules

Almost every such community is organised as an owners’ association: a propriedade horizontal or aldeamento in Portugal, a comunidad de propietarios in Spain. The association maintains roads, gates and shared facilities, sets rules on works and letting, and insures the common parts — with a policy written for the community, not for the individual house.

Resort apartments and rental programmes

Alongside the villas sit resort apartments and townhouses, often bought with a rental programme run by the resort operator. It suits owners who come for a few weeks a year, but turns a private home into a let property for much of the season, under an operator’s contract the owner may never have examined.

Staff, security and the practicalities

A house of this kind rarely runs itself: a gardener, a pool contractor, often a property manager who opens the house before each visit; a wine room, a buggy for the short drive to the first tee, solar panels and smart security. Each is a detail in daily life — and a line in a well-written policy.

Protecting the house, the collection and the golfer

A high-value home in a golf community tends to be insured on assumptions made at purchase and never revisited. These are the points that matter most.

  • Rebuild cost, not market value. The land and the view are expensive; the house must be insured for what it would cost to rebuild to the same specification — stone, joinery, glazing, landscaping — and architectural rules can raise that cost further.
  • Community policy versus your own. The association’s policy typically covers structure and common parts. Interiors, improvements, contents, valuables and your personal liability are yours to insure — and the boundary between the two should be read, not assumed.
  • Wildfire and storm. The Algarve hinterland and the hills of Andalusia are wildfire country in summer, and winter storms bring wind, hail and flash flooding. Vegetation management around the house matters to underwriters.
  • Long absences. Many golf homes stand empty for months, and standard policies often restrict cover after a period of unoccupancy; a policy designed for second homes avoids that trap.
  • Art, contents and valuables. Paintings, rugs, wine and watches in a second home belong on an agreed-value schedule, not inside a generic contents limit.
  • The golfer’s own liability. A sliced drive that injures another player or breaks a window on a fairway-side house is a personal liability claim. Family liability with a worldwide scope, and limits in the millions, follows the golfer from Vilamoura to St Andrews.
  • Clubs, buggies and events. Clubs in transit are best covered as personal possessions worldwide; a buggy driven by guests or off the course raises its own liability questions; and a charity day with a prize for a hole in one can be backed by prize indemnity cover.
  • Letting through the resort. If the house is let, the policy must know it; undisclosed letting is one of the most common reasons claims are contested.
  • Catastrophe cover differs by country. In Spain, extraordinary risks such as flood and earthquake are covered through the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros, funded by a surcharge on the policy. In Portugal there is no equivalent public scheme, and earthquake cover is an optional extension that has to be chosen deliberately — relevant in the Algarve, which sits in one of the country’s more seismically active zones.

For more detail, see our guides on what a golf resort’s own policy covers, homes in Quinta do Lago and Vale do Lobo, community insurance for owners in Spain and family liability limits.

A short checklist

  • The rebuild cost of the house has been assessed recently, including landscaping, pool and outbuildings.
  • You have read where the community policy stops and your own must begin.
  • Your policy knows how long the house stands empty and whether it is let.
  • Art, jewellery, watches and wine are listed at agreed values.
  • Your personal liability is worldwide, with a limit that reflects your assets.
  • In Portugal, you have decided consciously whether to add earthquake cover.

Frequently asked questions

Does the golf community’s insurance cover my villa?
Usually only in part. An owners’ association insures the common parts and sometimes the shell of the buildings. Interiors, improvements, contents, valuables and your personal liability are normally yours to insure; the community’s policy schedule shows where the line falls.
Should a house be insured for its purchase price?
No. The building should be insured for its rebuild cost to the same specification, which in a prime golf community can be well above or well below the market price. Land is not insured, and applying a standard rate per square metre to a bespoke house is a common cause of underinsurance.
Am I liable if my golf ball injures someone or damages a house?
Yes, you can be. A stray ball that hurts another player or breaks a window is a personal liability matter. A family liability policy with worldwide scope and a high limit is the usual answer, and it should also respond when you play abroad.
Is earthquake cover included in Portugal and Spain?
In Spain, extraordinary risks including earthquake and flood are covered through the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros, funded by a surcharge on the policy. In Portugal there is no equivalent public scheme; earthquake cover is an optional extension that must be requested.
Does letting my apartment through the resort programme affect my cover?
It can. Letting changes the risk, and the policy must be told. The operator’s contract also allocates responsibility for guests and damage, so it should be read alongside your own policy to avoid gaps or duplication.
