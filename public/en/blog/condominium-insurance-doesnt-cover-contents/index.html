What Your Condominium Insurance Doesn't Cover (and Why You Still Need Contents Cover)

Published 25 June 2026 · 8 min read
Hugo Gonçalves
Founder & Risk Management Specialist · Adler & Rochefort

Condominium insurance in Portugal is often misunderstood. It usually protects common building interests and legally required fire risk for the structure, but it does not automatically protect everything inside your apartment or every liability connected to your private use.

What condominium insurance usually focuses on

  • Common parts of the building, such as roof, stairs, entrance halls and shared systems.
  • The building structure according to insured capital and condominium rules.
  • Certain shared liabilities linked to the condominium.

What it may not cover for you

  • Your furniture, electronics, clothes, jewellery and personal belongings.
  • Improvements inside your unit that are not properly reflected in insured values.
  • Private liability for incidents inside your apartment.
  • Alternative accommodation or loss of rent unless specifically insured.
  • Damage caused by your contents or private installations to neighbours.

This gap matters after water damage, theft, fire smoke, electrical damage or a liability claim. The condominium policy may respond to the building, while your personal losses remain outside its scope.

The simple solution

Apartment owners should keep contents cover and private liability in addition to the condominium policy. The right setup depends on whether the unit is your main home, a second home or a rental property.

A worked example

A first-floor apartment in a Vilamoura block suffered a leak from a riser in the common wall. Water ran down the inside of the wall for a week before it showed. By the time it did, the plasterwork, the fitted wardrobes and a section of engineered oak floor were gone — about €9,400 of damage.

The condominium policy paid for the riser and for making good the structure. It did not pay for the wardrobes, the floor or the redecoration, because none of those is a common part. The owner had no policy of their own, on the reasonable-sounding assumption that the building was insured. The building was. The apartment was not.

Where the boundary sits

Article 1429 of the Civil Code makes fire insurance of the building compulsory and puts the obligation on the condominium for the common parts and on each owner for their own fraction. In practice the condominium takes out a single policy covering the whole structure, which is why owners conclude they are covered. What that policy insures is the structure and common parts — not what you have put inside your fraction, and not your liability as occupier.

What the insurer asks at underwriting

  • Whether a condominium policy is in force and what it covers — so the two do not overlap or leave a gap.
  • The contents sum insured you want, and whether any single item exceeds the per-item limit.
  • Whether the apartment is a permanent home, a second home or let.
  • The floor level, for water-damage exposure both incoming and outgoing.
  • Whether the fraction includes a garage, a storeroom or a terrace, and whether those are to be insured.

The three mistakes we see repeatedly

  • Never reading the condominium policy. Ask the administrator for the condições particulares. You are entitled to them, and they take ten minutes to read.
  • Assuming earthquake is included. A surprising number of condominium policies carry fire and little else. If the building policy has no seismic cover, neither does your fraction. See earthquake cover in the Algarve.
  • Carrying no liability cover. The most likely claim you will ever make as an apartment owner is damage to the flat below, and the condominium policy does not answer it.

What to do

Send us the condominium policy and we will tell you exactly where it stops, then quote the fraction policy that starts there. It is usually the cheapest home policy we write.

What a fraction policy costs

Because the structure is already insured by the condominium, a policy covering your own fraction is inexpensive. A typical Algarve apartment with €25,000 to €40,000 of contents, full liability and alternative accommodation runs between €110 and €220 a year. If you also want to insure the interior finishes and any improvements you have made — the fitted kitchen, the bathrooms, a reconfigured layout — add a modest building-improvements sum on top.

The one place to be careful is overlap. If the condominium policy already covers interior finishes, insuring them again pays you nothing extra and simply raises your premium. That is why we ask to see the condominium schedule before quoting: the objective is a policy that starts exactly where the building one stops, with no gap and no duplication.
