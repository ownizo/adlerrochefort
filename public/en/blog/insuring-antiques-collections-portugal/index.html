Insuring a collection that has no catalogue price

A collection presents a problem no single object does: the value is distributed, the pieces are frequently not individually documented, and much of what makes the collection worth what it is — completeness, condition, the fact that these particular pieces are together — does not appear in any price list. Insurers handle single objects well. They handle collections badly unless the arrangement is built deliberately.

The inventory is the whole job

Every other decision depends on having an inventory, and nearly every collection claim that goes badly goes badly for want of one. After a burglary or a fire, the burden of proving what existed sits with the owner. A recollection is not evidence; an itemised list with photographs, kept off-site, is.

What a workable inventory records, per item or per group: a description precise enough to identify it, dimensions, maker or period where known, condition and any restoration, the acquisition date, what was paid, and where it is kept. Two photographs — one general, one of marks, signatures or damage — are worth several paragraphs of description. Keep the file somewhere other than the building holding the collection, and date each revision.

The inventory is not paperwork for the insurer. It is the document that decides what you recover when the collection is no longer there to be examined.

Blanket cover, scheduling, or both

Collections are usually insured in one of two ways, and most sensible arrangements use both.

Blanket cover puts one sum over the whole collection with a stated cap on any single item. It suits collections of many broadly similar pieces — books, records, coins, ceramics, militaria — where individually scheduling three hundred items would cost more in administration than it returns. It flexes as the collection grows, within the sum.

Scheduling lists individual pieces with their own agreed sums and removes them from the per-item cap. Anything materially above the blanket cap belongs here, along with anything whose value rests on attribution or provenance rather than type.

The pair or set clause

This clause decides more collection claims than any other, and it is rarely read before it applies. It governs what happens when one part of a set is lost or damaged: a chair from a set of eight, one of a pair of candlesticks, a volume from a bound series, a single piece from a dinner service.

The restrictive version pays only the proportionate value of the part lost — one eighth of the set, for the chair — which is arithmetically tidy and commercially wrong, because seven chairs are worth far less than seven eighths of eight. The favourable version treats the loss as a total loss of the set, pays the full set value, and takes the remaining pieces in salvage. A middle version pays the loss in value to the set as a whole, assessed after the event.

Which one applies is a wording question, and it is negotiable at inception in a way it never is afterwards. If a collection contains sets — and most do — this is the clause to check first.

Restoration, condition and what is actually paid

For antiques, condition is value, and the two interact in ways that surprise people. Original condition, including original surface, is frequently worth more than restored condition, so a well-executed restoration can reduce the value of a piece rather than return it. A specialist wording handles this the same way an art wording does: it pays the restoration cost, and separately pays the loss in value that survives the restoration. A general contents section pays a repair invoice and stops.

Restoration invoices are also part of the value in their own right. A piece bought for €4,000 and restored for €9,000 is not a €4,000 item, and the only evidence of that is the invoice. Keep it with the inventory record.

Valuing what has no catalogue price

Some things have a published market: coins, stamps, wine, and to an extent furniture by known makers. Much of what sits in a real collection does not. The route through is a specialist valuation that shows its reasoning — comparable sales where they exist, replacement cost through the trade where they do not, and an explicit statement of the basis used. An underwriter will accept a well-argued figure from a recognised specialist and record it as the agreed value; that is precisely what agreed value is for. What an underwriter accepts as a valuation covers who can issue one in Portugal.

Where a collection appreciates, keep the figures moving. An out-of-date sum insured on a blanket collection exposes every partial claim to proportional reduction, not just the total loss.

Storage, security and movement

Underwriters price a collection by where it sits and how it is protected. Expect questions about which rooms hold what, whether display cases are locked and to what standard, the alarm grade and whether it covers the rooms in question, and whether anything is stored below ground, in an outbuilding, or off-site. Each location is declared and rated separately — including a bank vault, which is usually the cheapest place in the arrangement to hold anything not on display.

Two Portuguese specifics recur. Coastal humidity in the Algarve affects furniture, paper, textiles and gilt; gradual deterioration is excluded by every wording, so dehumidification is a maintenance cost rather than a claim. And second homes left empty for months engage the unoccupancy clause, which can suspend theft cover after a stated run of consecutive days — a serious matter when the contents are the reason for the policy.

Movement needs its own treatment. Taking pieces to a fair, lending to another collector, or sending something out for restoration all take the items outside the insured address, and none is automatically covered. Bringing a collection into Portugal in the first place is covered in moving a collection to Portugal.

Frequently asked questions

Can a collection be insured without listing every piece?
Yes, on a blanket basis: one sum for the whole collection, with a stated cap on any single item. It suits collections of many similar low-value pieces — books, records, ceramics — and it still requires an inventory, because after a burglary you have to prove what was there. Items above the per-item cap are scheduled individually alongside the blanket sum.
What does the pair or set clause actually do?
It sets out what the insurer pays when one part of a set is lost or damaged. The favourable version treats the loss as a total loss of the set on surrender of the remainder; the restrictive version pays only the proportionate value of the lost part, leaving you with an incomplete set and a fraction of the money. Which version applies is a wording question worth settling before inception.
How do you value something with no comparable sales?
Through a specialist rather than a price guide, and by documenting the reasoning: comparable sales where any exist, replacement cost through the trade, restoration invoices, and provenance. Where genuinely no comparable exists, underwriters will accept a well-argued figure from a recognised specialist and record it as the agreed value.
Does the policy cover items bought after inception?
Specialist wordings normally include automatic cover for newly acquired items for 30 to 90 days, capped at a percentage of the existing sum insured, on condition you notify within the period. For an actively growing collection that condition is easy to breach — a standing habit of reporting acquisitions at each renewal, or as they happen, is the practical fix.
Is a collection covered while displayed at a fair or club event?
Not automatically. Cover follows the insured address unless the wording extends it. Exhibiting, showing at a club, or lending to another collector all need to be declared; some wordings include a limited exhibition extension, others require notification each time. Transit to and from the event is a separate question again.
What if items are stored in more than one place?
Each location has to be declared and rated separately, including a bank vault or commercial store. Underwriters price by what is where, and an undeclared second location is a straightforward route to a reduced settlement. It also matters for security conditions, which are set per location, not per policy.
