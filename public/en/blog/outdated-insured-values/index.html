When the insured value stops matching the thing insured

Published 6 August 2026 · 11 min read
Hugo Gonçalves
Founder & Risk Management Specialist · Adler & Rochefort

Underinsurance is not a decision anyone makes. It happens by standing still: a figure set once, renewed automatically, while construction costs, a collection and a currency all move underneath it. In Portugal the consequence is arithmetic, and it is applied to partial claims as well as total ones.

The proportional rule, in one paragraph

Portuguese policies apply the proportional rule (regra proporcional, the average clause in English practice). If the sum insured is lower than the true value of the insured property, the insurer settles claims in the same proportion. Insure a €1,000,000 rebuild for €600,000 and you are 60 per cent insured; a €100,000 water-damage claim is settled at €60,000, less the excess. The claim was well inside the sum insured, and it was still cut. This is contractual arithmetic, not a claims-handling attitude, and it applies to buildings, contents, stock, equipment and collections alike.

The premium was calculated on the figure you declared. The settlement is calculated on the same figure. Nothing in the process notices the gap until the loss adjuster measures it.

Why the figure drifts

  • Construction costs. Rebuild cost is labour, materials and professional fees at today's prices. It has moved substantially over the last five years, and a figure set before that period understates the rebuild even if nothing about the house has changed.
  • The house changed. A pool, an annexe, a new kitchen, a solar array, a converted basement — each adds rebuild cost, and none of them updates the policy on its own.
  • Things appreciated. Art, watches, jewellery with significant gold or stone content, classic cars and wine do not depreciate on a schedule. Some of them have doubled while sitting still.
  • Things were bought. The single most common cause: an acquisition joins the household and never joins the schedule.
  • A restoration completed. A car or a piece of furniture part-way through work is worth less than the finished object. The insured value should follow the work, not the invoice date.
  • Currency. A collection valued in sterling or dollars and insured in euros moves whenever the rate does.

What indexation does, and what it does not

Most Portuguese policies apply an automatic indexation clause, uplifting the sums insured each year by a published index. It is useful and it is not sufficient. Indexation tracks general inflation; it does not know that you finished a basement conversion, bought a painting, or that the model of watch in the safe has trebled at auction. It also cannot correct a starting figure that was wrong: an index applied to an understated sum produces a slightly larger understated sum.

Two other mechanisms are worth asking about by name. Some specialist wordings waive the proportional rule outright, or apply it only where the under-declaration exceeds a stated tolerance — typically 10 or 15 per cent. Others write scheduled items on an agreed value basis, which removes the argument for those items entirely because the figure is fixed in the contract in advance. Neither is a reason to leave the schedule out of date, but both change what an honest mistake costs.

Where over-insurance is the problem instead

The rule does not work in reverse. Insuring a €600,000 rebuild for €1,000,000 does not produce a larger settlement — indemnity is capped at the loss actually suffered — it simply produces a larger premium every year. Over-declaration is common on properties insured at purchase price rather than rebuild cost, and on modern cars still insured at what they cost new. It is quieter than underinsurance and it is not free.

A renewal that is actually a review

Once a year, before the renewal date rather than after it:

  1. Compare the buildings figure against current construction costs, and add anything built or installed since.
  2. Walk the contents schedule against the house. Add what arrived, remove what left.
  3. Reprice the scheduled items whose class is due — the table above tells you which.
  4. Check the aggregate at each address against what the insurer agreed to accept there.
  5. Confirm the conditions still describe reality: occupancy, security, who lives there, whether anything is let.
  6. Ask, in writing, whether the proportional rule applies to this policy and with what tolerance.

The same discipline applies to a business: stock, equipment and buildings drift for identical reasons, and the rule is the same one. What differs is only how fast the figures move.

Frequently asked questions

Does the proportional rule apply to partial claims too?
Yes, and that is the part owners find hardest to believe. The reduction is applied to whatever the loss is, in the ratio of the declared sum to the true value. A claim that is a small fraction of the sum insured is still cut by the same percentage as a total loss would be.
Does an indexation clause keep the policy up to date?
Only against general inflation. It cannot account for works carried out, items acquired, or a collection appreciating faster than the index — and applied to a figure that was wrong at inception it simply inflates the error. Treat it as maintenance, not as a substitute for reviewing the figures.
Does agreed value remove the problem?
For the items written on that basis, yes: the figure is fixed in the contract at inception on the strength of a valuation, and the settlement is that figure. It does not help the building sum insured or the unscheduled contents, which remain exposed to the rule, and the agreed figure itself still has to be revisited as values move.
What tolerance do insurers allow before the rule bites?
It varies by wording. Some apply the rule strictly from the first euro of under-declaration; several specialist wordings apply it only beyond a stated margin, commonly 10 or 15 per cent, and a few waive it altogether. It is a question worth asking in writing before the policy is bound, because the answer materially changes the risk of an honest mistake.
What happens if I insured the house for what I paid for it?
You are almost certainly over-insured on the building and paying for it every year, because the purchase price includes land — often most of it on a coastal plot — and land does not need rebuilding. The corrected figure is the construction cost of putting the same building back, and it is usually a good deal lower than the price paid.
Who is supposed to notice that the values have drifted?
Contractually, the policyholder: the duty to declare the value accurately sits with the insured, and an automatic renewal notice is not a review. In practice it is the job of whoever handles the renewal to raise it, which is why an annual review against the schedule — rather than a renewal notice paid without reading — is the whole of the answer.
