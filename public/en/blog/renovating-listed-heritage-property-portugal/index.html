Renovating a listed or heritage property in Portugal: insurance during and after the works

July 2026 · Adler & Rochefort

Portugal is full of buildings worth saving: a quinta with centuries of history behind Loulé, a townhouse in the historic centre of a walled village, an old farmhouse with thick stone walls and hand-made tiles, a manor that has passed through several families. Foreign owners buy these properties precisely because they are not new, and then set about a renovation that can run for a year or more. It is during that period — the building open, scaffolded, half its roof off, trades coming and going — that the property is most exposed and, very often, least well insured. This article sets out how a heritage or listed renovation in Portugal should be covered while the work is under way, and how the cover has to change once it is finished.

Why a home policy does not cover a building site

The first and most consequential misunderstanding is that the existing home insurance carries on through the works. A Portuguese multirriscos home policy is written for a finished, occupied dwelling. Its assumptions — that the building is intact, that it is lived in, that it is not surrounded by scaffolding and open to the weather — are exactly the conditions a renovation suspends. Once major or structural work begins, most home policies restrict or exclude cover, both because the property is a building site and because it is usually unoccupied while the work runs. Relying on the home policy through a renovation frequently means the building is, in practice, uninsured for the very period it is most at risk.

A renovation is a different insurance problem and needs cover built for it. Broadly, the works are protected through construction cover — often called contractors' or construction all-risks — which responds to damage to the works and materials during the project, alongside the liability the site creates. The home policy then resumes, or is re-set on new terms, once the property is complete and habitable again. Treating the project as a phase with its own insurance, rather than as an interruption the home policy will somehow absorb, is the foundation everything else rests on. Our note on insurance for construction and building works covers the mechanics in more detail.

Insuring the works, the site and the existing structure

A heritage renovation carries three exposures at once, and good cover addresses all three rather than one. There is the work itself — the new materials, the labour already put in, the part-completed structure — which can be damaged by fire, storm, water or collapse before it is finished. There is the existing building, the historic fabric you are working within, which is at risk from the works themselves: a fire during hot works on an old timber roof, a collapse when a wall is opened up, water ingress once the roof is off. And there is liability, because a building site with an open frontage onto a village street is a place where passers-by, neighbours and visitors can be hurt or their property damaged.

The existing structure is the exposure owners most often leave uninsured, because they think of construction cover as protecting only the new work. On a heritage project the value at risk in the old fabric — the very thing that made the property worth buying — can far exceed the value of the works. Cover needs to be arranged so that the historic building is protected against damage arising during the project, not just the new materials stacked in the courtyard. Getting the split right between what the contractor insures and what the owner must insure is central, and it is the point on which the largest gaps open.

On a heritage renovation the greatest value is not the new work but the old walls you are working within — and those are exactly the ones a construction policy does not automatically protect.

Where the builder's cover ends and yours begins

In Portugal a contractor undertaking building work is expected to carry their own insurance: liability cover for the work they do, and the compulsory workplace-accidents insurance (seguro de acidentes de trabalho) that protects their workforce if someone is injured on site. This is real and important cover, but it protects the contractor's position, not the owner's. It does not automatically insure the owner's interest in the building, the value tied up in the existing structure, or the owner's own liability as the person who commissioned the project and owns the site.

The practical step, before a single tool is lifted, is to establish in writing what the builder's policies actually cover, for how much, and until when — and then to arrange the owner's own cover to fill what remains. A verbal assurance that “the builder is insured” is not a substitute for seeing the policy and understanding its limits. Where a project is run by an architect and several trades rather than a single main contractor, the question of who insures what becomes more tangled still, and the owner's cover has to be positioned deliberately so that nothing important is left to an assumption that turned out to be wrong.

The extra cost of restoring protected fabric

Heritage and listed buildings break the ordinary link between the size of a building and the cost of putting it right, and this is where sums insured go wrong. Reinstating protected or historic fabric is not the same as building new: it means traditional materials, specialist trades who work in stone or lime or period timber, elements that have to be hand-made or matched rather than bought off the shelf, and often a requirement to restore like-for-like rather than replace with a modern equivalent. All of that costs materially more than constructing a standard house of the same footprint.

If the sum insured is set to what a modern building of that size would cost, a heritage property is underinsured against the reality of how it would actually have to be repaired. In Portugal that gap has teeth: under the proportional rule (regra proporcional), a property insured for less than its correct reinstatement value can have even a partial claim settled in the same proportion. A heritage building insured on a modern-build basis, and then damaged, can find the claim cut precisely because the true cost of period restoration was never reflected. Both during and after the works, these buildings need to be valued on a realistic reinstatement basis — and, like any property, revisited as costs move, since sums insured left unchanged for years are a frequent weakness.

Unoccupied, and open to the elements

Renovations run for months, and for much of that time the property stands empty, often partly open to the weather. Both facts change the risk and both are conditions insurers care about. An unoccupied building accumulates damage nobody notices: a leak that runs for weeks, an intrusion, a small fire that spreads before anyone is there to see it. A building with its roof off or its windows out is exposed to storms and water in a way a sealed one is not. Cover for a renovation has to be arranged with the unoccupied, exposed state of the property in mind, rather than on terms that quietly assume someone is living there.

Security and the sequencing of the works feed directly into this. A site left open, unsecured and unattended over a long weekend is a different risk from one that is fenced, locked and checked, and the terms of the cover — and the response to a claim — can reflect that. None of this is a reason not to renovate; it is a reason to insure the project as the extended, exposed, unoccupied phase it genuinely is, and to keep the insurer informed as the state of the building changes through the job.

Resetting the cover once the works are done

The end of a renovation is not the end of the insurance question — it is the moment the home cover has to be put back on the right footing, and it is easy to let it lapse into an old, wrong shape. A property that has just been restored is usually worth more, and costs more to rebuild, than it did before the works. The reinstatement value has changed, the building may now contain finishes and systems it did not have before, and the sum insured on the home policy has to be re-set to reflect the property as it now stands rather than as it was bought.

This is also the point to make sure the finished building carries the cover a home of its standing needs. A restored quinta or manor is frequently a higher-value property with contents, features and liability exposures to match, and it may be better served by a policy written for higher-value homes than by whatever standard cover was in place before. Moving cleanly from the construction phase to a correctly-sized home policy, at the moment the works complete, is what ensures the effort and money poured into the restoration are actually protected once you are living in it.

Getting the sequence right

A heritage renovation insured well follows a simple sequence, even if the building work is anything but simple. Before the works start, the existing home cover is reviewed and the construction phase is insured in its own right — the works, the existing historic fabric, the site liability — with a clear, written understanding of where the contractor's cover ends and the owner's begins. Through the works, the property is insured on terms that acknowledge it is unoccupied and exposed, and the insurer is kept informed as the state of the building changes. And when the project completes, the home policy is re-set to the finished property's reinstatement value, on cover suited to what it has become.

Owners who follow that sequence rarely have a bad surprise; the ones who come unstuck are almost always the ones who assumed a single policy would carry them from purchase through building work to completion. It will not. A building worth restoring is worth insuring for the way it actually has to be restored — and for the period, during the works, when it is most vulnerable of all.
