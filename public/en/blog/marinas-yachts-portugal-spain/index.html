Marine & Private Clients

Marinas and yachts in Portugal and Spain: from the Atlantic to the Balearics

Published 27 September 2026 · 8 min read
Hugo Gonçalves
Founder & Risk Management Specialist · Adler & Rochefort

The Iberian peninsula is, for a yachtsman, two very different seas joined at Gibraltar. To the west lies the Atlantic: long swells, the Portuguese trade winds, the islands of Madeira and the Azores, and the great ocean routes to the Caribbean. To the east lies the Mediterranean: the Balearics, the Costa Brava, the summer anchorages and a superyacht season that sets the tone for Europe. Portugal and Spain between them offer both, with marinas of real quality at every step.

This guide follows that coastline — the harbours, the sailing, the homes by the water — and ends with what an owner needs to think about when the boat and the house become part of the same household.

Portugal: an Atlantic sailing nation

Portugal’s maritime history needs no introduction, and its modern sailing life is shaped by the same ocean. The prevailing northerlies of summer — the nortada — make the passage south along the coast a downwind run, which is why so many northern European yachts come through Portuguese marinas on their way to the Mediterranean or across the Atlantic.

The Algarve

Vilamoura is the country’s best-known marina, large and well-served, with a boatyard, restaurants on the quay and apartments overlooking the berths. To the west, Portimão sits at the mouth of the Arade river and has hosted international regattas, and Lagos, with its marina behind the old town and the Ponta da Piedade just outside, is a favourite departure point for boats heading to Madeira or the Canaries. The Algarve’s appeal is the season: sailing is possible almost all year.

Lisbon, Cascais and Tróia

Cascais has a long tradition of sail racing and the marina is at the heart of a town that has hosted the America’s Cup World Series and major offshore events. In Lisbon itself, the Doca de Alcântara sits beneath the 25 de Abril bridge, and the Marina do Parque das Nações lies upriver on the wide Tagus estuary, one of the finest natural harbours in Europe. Across the Sado, the marina at Tróia gives access to the dolphins of the estuary and to the long beaches that run south towards Comporta.

Madeira and the Azores

Out in the Atlantic, Funchal and Calheta in Madeira are the natural first landfall for boats leaving the mainland for the south. And Horta, on the island of Faial in the Azores, is one of the great ports of ocean sailing: the stopover for yachts returning from the Caribbean to Europe, where crews still paint their boat’s name and dates on the harbour wall and meet at Peter Café Sport before the last leg home.

The Atlantic crossing

For many owners the ultimate voyage is westward. The Atlantic Rally for Cruisers — the ARC — leaves Las Palmas de Gran Canaria each November for the Caribbean, and many of its boats reach the Canaries through Portugal and Madeira. The route explains why an owner based in Lisbon or the Algarve may, in the same year, need cover for a Mediterranean summer, an ocean passage and a Caribbean winter.

Spain: from the Balearics to the Canaries

Mallorca and Ibiza

Palma de Mallorca is the capital of Mediterranean yachting. Club de Mar, at the foot of the old town, and the Real Club Náutico de Palma, organiser of the Copa del Rey regatta, sit a short walk apart; to the west, Puerto Portals is the island’s most social harbour and Port Adriano, redesigned by Philippe Starck, takes some of the largest yachts in the Mediterranean. Palma is also a centre of refit and yacht services, which is why so many large yachts winter there. In Ibiza, Marina Ibiza and Ibiza Magna face the old walled town across the harbour, with Formentera an hour away.

The Costa del Sol

Puerto Banús, opened in 1970, remains the emblem of Marbella’s glamour, its quay lined with some of the largest yachts that visit the southern Spanish coast. Further west, the marina at Sotogrande is quieter and residential — the waterfront half of an estate whose golf we describe in a separate article — with homes and apartments set directly on the canals.

Barcelona, Valencia and the Costa Brava

Barcelona’s OneOcean Port Vell sits at the foot of the old city and hosted the 37th America’s Cup in 2024; Valencia hosted the event in 2007 and 2010 and still carries the legacy in its port. North of Barcelona, the Costa Brava offers coves and small harbours from Palamós to Roses, with the Pyrenees behind and France within a day’s sail.

The Canary Islands

Las Palmas de Gran Canaria is where the ARC begins, and the islands as a whole are an Atlantic sailing ground with steady trade winds and winter warmth. Many owners keep a boat in the Canaries all year, using the islands as a base for passages to Madeira, the Cape Verde islands and beyond.

The homes: living by the water

For many owners the boat and the house come as a pair. The most sought-after properties sit on the waterfront: villas facing the sea on the cliffs of the Algarve or the coast of Mallorca, marina apartments overlooking the berths at Vilamoura, Sotogrande, Puerto Portals or Port Adriano, and houses on the canals of Sotogrande with the boat moored at the end of the garden.

A few properties have their own private mooring or jetty, where planning and maritime rules allow it — rare on these coasts and highly valued. Berths themselves are a separate matter: in both countries a marina berth is usually held as a long-term concession or right of use granted by the marina operator under a public concession, rather than owned outright, and the terms of that contract — duration, transfer, obligations, insurance requirements — deserve the same attention as a title deed.

Waterfront living has its own practicalities: salt air that corrodes metalwork, air conditioning and electrics; exposure to wind and wave during winter storms; ground-floor apartments close to the water table; and, for houses with a jetty or pontoon, a liability that extends onto the water. Many of these homes are also second residences, occupied in the season and closed for months at a time.

Protecting the yacht, the house and the family

A yacht is insured on its own policy, and a waterfront home on another, but the two meet at the same household — and the gaps tend to appear where they meet.

  • Hull and machinery at an agreed value. The yacht should be insured for a value agreed at inception, supported by a survey, so that a total loss is paid without argument over depreciation.
  • Third-party liability. Liability cover for recreational craft is compulsory in both Portugal and Spain, and marinas typically require limits well above the legal minimum before they will allocate a berth. Wreck removal and pollution deserve a close look, as they are frequently sub-limited.
  • Crew and skipper. A professional skipper or crew brings employer obligations, including accident cover and repatriation. Commercial yachts must also comply with the Maritime Labour Convention.
  • Private use or charter. A yacht used privately and one chartered to third parties are different risks. Charter requires commercial registration and cover; using a privately insured yacht for paid charter is one of the surest ways to lose a claim.
  • Cruising area and season. Navigation limits must match the real itinerary — Mediterranean, Atlantic islands or an ocean crossing — and many policies restrict cover in the Caribbean during the hurricane season. A declared winter lay-up ashore should be recorded, not assumed.
  • Tenders, jet skis and water toys. These are often excluded or limited unless listed, and those used by guests or children raise liability questions of their own.
  • Personal effects aboard. Clothes, electronics, watches and art on board are rarely covered by the hull policy in full; worldwide personal possessions cover fills the gap.
  • The waterfront home. Storm, flood and salt damage, the jetty or pontoon and the liability that goes with it, and long periods of vacancy all belong in the home policy. In Spain, extraordinary risks such as flood and storm surge are covered through the Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros; in Portugal there is no equivalent public scheme, so the scope of storm, flood and earthquake cover depends on the policy chosen.

For more detail, see our guides on boat insurance in Portugal, what each Algarve marina requires, private use versus charter and lay-up and the winter months.

A short checklist

  • The yacht is insured at an agreed value supported by a recent survey.
  • Liability limits meet what your marina actually requires, including wreck removal.
  • Navigation limits match the real itinerary for the coming year.
  • Crew, charter use and water toys are declared.
  • Your berth contract has been read for its insurance obligations.
  • The waterfront home is covered for storm, flood and its jetty or pontoon.

Frequently asked questions

Is yacht liability insurance compulsory in Portugal and Spain?
Yes. Both countries require third-party liability cover for recreational craft, and marinas usually ask for limits well above the legal minimum before they allocate a berth. The exact requirement depends on the vessel and the marina contract.
Do I own my marina berth?
Usually not outright. In both countries a berth is typically held as a long-term concession or right of use granted by the marina operator, which itself holds a public concession. The contract sets the duration, transfer rules and insurance obligations.
Can I charter my yacht on a private policy?
No. Charter to paying guests requires commercial registration and commercial cover. Using a privately insured yacht for paid charter is a common reason claims are declined.
Is my yacht covered on an Atlantic crossing to the Caribbean?
Only if the navigation limits allow it. Ocean passages and Caribbean cruising usually have to be agreed in advance, and many policies restrict or exclude named-storm damage in the Caribbean during the hurricane season.
Does my home insurance cover the jetty or pontoon?
Not automatically. A private jetty or pontoon, and the liability to people using it, should be declared and insured explicitly, together with storm and flood cover suited to a waterfront property.
