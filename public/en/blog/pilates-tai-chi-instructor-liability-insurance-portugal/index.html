Liability insurance for Pilates and Tai Chi instructors in Portugal

Published 13 September 2026 · 6 min read
Hugo Gonçalves
Founder & Risk Management Specialist · Adler & Rochefort

Pilates and Tai Chi sit outside the seven practices Lei n.º 71/2013 formally regulates, which means no ACSS licence and no statutory insurance minimum attach to teaching either one. The exposure is nonetheless real and, in practice, often looks more like a fitness-instruction risk than a complementary-therapy one.

Two disciplines, similar underlying exposure

Pilates instruction frequently involves equipment — reformers, Cadillac frames, resistance springs — that introduces mechanical injury risk beyond what mat-based classes alone would carry, plus hands-on correction of a student's form. Tai Chi is lower-impact and rarely equipment-heavy, but still involves physical instruction, balance work that can matter for older or less mobile students, and group classes where a fall or collision is a real, if uncommon, possibility. Neither is one of the seven therapies Lei n.º 71/2013 lists, and neither carries the Article 10.º insurance obligation or the Portaria n.º 200/2014 minimum that Acupuncture, Phytotherapy, Homeopathy, Traditional Chinese Medicine, Naturopathy, Osteopathy and Chiropractic do.

What actually drives the risk

  • Equipment, for Pilates specifically. Reformer and apparatus-based classes carry mechanical risk that mat work does not, and studio insurance for the premises does not automatically extend to the instructor's own professional liability.
  • Hands-on correction, for both. Physically adjusting a student's form or posture is a different exposure from verbal cueing alone.
  • Older and less mobile students. Tai Chi in particular is often taught to older adults for balance and mobility, which raises both the standard of care expected and the consequences of a fall.
  • Where you teach. A dedicated studio, a room inside a gym or wellness centre, a park or public space for Tai Chi, and private sessions each carry a different premises picture on top of the instruction risk itself.

The contractual reality

As with yoga, the absence of a legal requirement does not mean the market has stopped asking. Studios, gyms and wellness centres increasingly require instructors — Pilates and Tai Chi included — to hold their own liability cover before teaching on their premises, as a condition of the booking rather than the law.

Frequently asked questions

Do Pilates and Tai Chi instructors need liability insurance by law in Portugal?
Not under Lei n.º 71/2013 — neither is one of the seven regulated complementary therapies, so there is no statutory minimum insurance figure. Individual studios, gyms or platforms may still require cover as a condition of teaching there.
Is Pilates riskier to insure than Tai Chi?
The exposures differ rather than one being simply higher — Pilates often involves equipment that introduces mechanical risk, while Tai Chi's risk profile leans more on balance, group movement and, often, an older student base. A policy should reflect which you actually teach, and how.
Does studio insurance already cover me as an instructor?
Not necessarily. Premises or public-liability cover held by a studio typically protects the studio itself, not automatically the individual instructor's own professional liability — confirm what the studio's policy actually covers rather than assuming it extends to you.
I teach both Pilates and yoga — do I need two policies?
Not necessarily two separate policies, but the cover needs to reflect the actual combination of activities you teach, including any equipment used. See our page on yoga instructor liability insurance for the adjacent case.
