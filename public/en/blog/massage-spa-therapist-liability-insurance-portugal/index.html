Liability insurance for massage and spa therapists in Portugal

Published 13 September 2026 · 6 min read
Hugo Gonçalves
Founder & Risk Management Specialist · Adler & Rochefort

Massage therapist is not one of the seven practices Lei n.º 71/2013 recognises as a complementary therapy, and it is a genuinely different activity from physiotherapy, osteopathy or the regulated therapies — each of which has its own legal framework and its own insurance solution. Getting that distinction right is the starting point for insuring the activity correctly.

Not one of the seven, and not another profession either

It is worth being precise about what "massage therapist" is not: not a physiotherapist, not an osteopath, not a practitioner of one of the seven recognised therapies under Lei n.º 71/2013, and not an aesthetics professional. Each of those carries its own legal framework, its own subscription criteria with an insurer, and its own insurance solution — and someone practising more than one of them needs a policy that reflects that actual combination, not one written for a single activity while others are quietly left out.

Different types of massage, different risk

Therapeutic and deep-tissue work carries a different injury profile from relaxation massage — more pressure, more targeted work near joints and soft tissue, more potential to aggravate an existing condition the client did not disclose or the therapist did not detect. Where sports massage, pregnancy massage or work with clients recovering from injury is involved, the standard of care expected rises accordingly.

The workplace changes the exposure

Working from your own premises, inside a third-party clinic or spa, or travelling to a client's home are genuinely different situations for liability purposes. A home visit in particular adds a premises/operations exposure that does not exist in the same way inside a fixed, controlled space you or an employer controls — access, the client's own environment, and factors outside your control all enter the picture.

What to check before engaging a massage therapist, or as one

  • What is actually being practised. "Massage" covers a wide range of techniques and intensities — the policy should match what is actually done, not a generic label.
  • Where it happens. Own premises, third-party clinic or spa, and client's home each carry a different exposure and may need to be reflected explicitly.
  • Whether other activities are combined. Aesthetics treatments, a recognised complementary therapy, or physiotherapy-adjacent work alongside massage each add their own requirements.
  • What the venue itself requires. Spas and hotels increasingly require their own proof of cover from therapists working on their premises, contractually rather than by law.
[VERIFY] No specific minimum capital figure is stated for massage-therapist liability cover on this page, because massage is not one of the seven therapies with a statutory minimum under Portaria n.º 200/2014 — confirm what a specific spa, hotel or clinic requires directly.

Frequently asked questions

Is massage therapy legally required to carry liability insurance in Portugal?
No, not under Lei n.º 71/2013 — massage therapy is not one of the seven regulated complementary therapies, so there is no statutory minimum insurance figure. Individual spas, hotels or clinics may still require it as a condition of working there.
Is a massage therapist the same as a physiotherapist or osteopath, for insurance purposes?
No — each is a distinct activity with its own legal framework and its own insurance solution. If you practise more than one, the policy needs to reflect that actual combination rather than covering only one of them.
Does working from a client's home change what cover I need?
It changes the exposure, yes — a home visit adds a premises/operations risk that does not exist in the same way inside a fixed, controlled space, so it is worth confirming the policy reflects where you actually work, not just one setting.
Do spas and hotels require their own proof of insurance from therapists?
Increasingly, yes, as a contractual condition of working on their premises — separate from any legal requirement, which does not exist for massage the way it does for the seven regulated therapies.
